Herein, we achieved production of the dianion of PBI-Bis-2AN (PBI2@) by a photochemical method, that is, by photoirradiation of a mixture of PBI-Bis-2-AN and a sacrificial electron donor (TEOA). Previously, PBIC@ was obtained by a chemical reduction method,[94, 96, 97] an electrochemical method,[78, 92, 93]
or a photochemical method.[95, 98, 99] Only limited studies are
available on the formation of PBI2@. Previously, it was obtained
by chemical reduction with, for example, dithionite
(Na2S2O4).[95, 100]
Although it was reported that PBI2@ can be formed by photoexcitation of PBIC@ in the presence of a sacrificial electron
donor,[95] the short excited-state lifetime of PBIC@ (145 : 15 ps)
is unfavorable for this photo-driven intermolecular electron
transfer.[94a] Majima et al. showed that photoexcited PBIC@ can
act as an electron acceptor to form PBI2@, but this was an intramolecular electron transfer.[101, 102] Therefore, whether or not
the PBI2@ can be formed through intermolecular electron
transfer by photoexcitation of PBIC@ , especially by photoexcitation at the near-IR absorption band, is still doubtful.
To elucidate the mechanism of generation of PBI2@, we studied the formation of PBI2@ in our systems. After production of
PBIC@ , we selectively photoirradiated the PBIC@ solution at
730 nm with an LED lamp (wavelength range: ca. 700–750 nm,
centered at 730 nm, light power density at the solution position: 50 mW cm@2 ; note that the solution of neutral PBI-Bis-2AN shows absorption at < 561 nm). Interestingly, we did not
find any formation of PBI2@ (Figure S34 in the Supporting Information). On photoirradiation of the PBI-Bis-2-AN/PBIC@-Bis-2AN mixed solution at 520 nm, however, formation of PBI2@ was
observed (Figure S35 in the Supporting Information; light
power density at the solution position: 50 mW cm@2). Therefore, we suggest that the formation of PBI2@ by photoexcitation of PBIC@/PBI mixed solution in the presence of a sacrificial
electron donor is probably due to the promotion of an electron of PBIC@ from, for instance, its HOMOa to LUMO + 4a
(Table S2 and Figure S42 in the Supporting Information, that is,
the D0 !D10 transition, not the D0 !D4 transition, which shows
700 nm absorption). Recently, photoexcited arene radical
anions were proposed as stronger reductants in photocatalysis,
due to the long absorption wavelength and the higher reduction potential of the radical compared with the neutral chromophore.[94] However, herein our results show that selective
photoexcitation into the near-IR absorption band of the D0 !
D4 transition (ca. 700 nm, with monochromic light) of PBIC@
does not drive a further reduction to produce PBI2@ (through
intermolecular electron transfer; we assume the same results
for D0 !D1/D2/D3, which show absorption at longer wavelength). Recently, consecutive charging of substituted PBI by
photoreduction to produce PBI and PBI2@ was reported, but

The mechanism of formation of PBI2@ from PBIC@ is supposed to be reduction of photoexcited PBIC@ , with either PBIC@

Conclusion
We prepared PBI-AN compact electron donor–acceptor dyads/
triad to study the effect of molecular conformation and chromophore orientation on the photophysical properties, especially the SOCT-ISC efficiency. The molecular conformation was
varied by changing the substitution position of the AN moiety
attached at the bay position of the PBI moiety. Weaker
ground-state electronic coupling between the PBI and AN
moieties was observed in the dyad with substitution at the 9position of the AN moiety, which adopts nearly orthogonal geometry. In contrast, in the dyad and triad with substitution at
the 2-position of the AN unit, a more coplanar geometry resulted, as well as stronger ground-state coupling and redshifted absorption. The orthogonal dyad shows more efficient triplet population and high singlet-oxygen generation (FD = 86 %)
compared with the non-orthogonal dyad (FD = 57 %). All the
dyads/triad show the long-lived PBI-localized triplet state (tT =
139 ms). Femtosecond transient absorption spectra show that
the charge separation of PBI-2-AN takes 3.6 ps, and the charge
recombination (i.e., SOCT-ISC) occurs within 2.8 ns.

All the PBI derivatives exhibit efficient reversible
formation of the near IR-absorbing PBI radical anion (PBIC@ , absorptions bands centered at 708, 802, 864, and 954 nm) on
photoreduction in the presence of a sacrificial electron donor
(TEOA). For PBI-AN triad, we observed reversible stepwise photoreduction of the PBI moiety in the triad to the radical anion
(PBIC@) and dianion (PBI2@) in the presence of TEOA. PBI2@ is
potent reductant (oxidation potential of PBI2@ : @1.29 V vs. Fc/
Fc + ) and could be used in photovoltaics and photoredox catalytic synthetic organic reactions. A detailed mechanistic study
on the stepwise photoreduction with monochromatic photoirradiation indicated that selective photoexcitation of PBIC@ at
the near-IR absorption band (centered at 740 nm), mainly D0 !

General procedures
All chemicals used were of analytical grade and used as received.
UV/Vis absorption spectra were measured with a UV-2550 UV/Vis
spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Ltd., Japan). Fluorescence emission
spectra were obtained with an RF-5301PC spectrofluorometer (Shimadzu Ltd., Japan). Fluorescence excitation spectra were recorded
with an FS5 spectrofluorometer, operated in photon-counting detection mode (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd, UK). Fluorescence lifetimes were measured with an OB920 luminescence lifetime spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd, UK) TCSPC detection mode,
510 nm picosecond pulsed laser, the instrument response function
(IRF) was 100 ps; repetition rates of the EPL picosecond laser for
different lifetime ranges were 20 MHz for 50 ns detection window,
10 MHz for 100 ns detection window, and 5 MHz for 200 ns of detection window).
