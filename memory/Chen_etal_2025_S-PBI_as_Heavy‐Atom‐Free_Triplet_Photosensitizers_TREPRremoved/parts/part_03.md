These negatively charged species are in particular of
interests because the photoexcited states of these monoanion
and dianions are super strong reductants in photocatalytic
redox organic reactions. However, a road blocks for these
photoexcited anions to be used in photocatalysis is their shortexcited state lifetimes. For the monoanions, the excited state
(doublet state) lifetimes are on the picoseconds magnitude.
For the dianions, with closed shell electronic configuration,
the excited state (normally singlet excited state) lifetimes
are on the scales of a few nanoseconds. These excited state
lifetimes are too short for molecular diffusion in fluid solution
at a reasonable concentration.
Thus, using CW EPR, we have observed a correlation
between g values and the number of S atoms in the molecule.
Larger deviation of the g factor from that of the free electron
implies a larger contribution of SOC effect. Thus, we observe
that the incorporation of additional S atoms increases the
SOC within the compound. Notably, S substitution at distinct
positions renders the two N atoms in the PBI molecule
nonequivalent, and this nonuniform spin density distribution
is capable of augmenting the chemical reactivity of the
molecule. Further details on how this affects the ISC can be
found in the section of theoretical study (see below).
In order to fully exploit the advantages of the super
strong reductant potential of the anions, herein, we studied
the formation of the monoanions and the dianions of the
thionated PBI derivatives, and we found the ISC ability for
the dianions.

Previous theoretical analysis based on natural bond orbital
(NBO) analysis show that there is a poor overlap between
the lone pairs of the sulfur and the carbon atoms, and as a
result, the antibonding character of π *C = S is weaker than
that of π *C = O , leading to a lower LUMO energy level for
the thiocarbonyl group than for the carbonyl group.[66] This
effect contributes to the anodically shifted reduction potential
of the thionated PBI derivatives as compared to the oxo
counterpart, as well as for the redshifted absorption bands of
the thionated PBI derivatives.

(SOCMEs), and their derivatives with respect to the normal
coordinates as input. In both cases, the Franck–Condon (FC)
contribution as well as the Herzberg–Teller (HT) contribution
was calculated.
It has been shown that HT contributions are the main
driving force of ISC processes occurring where the transition
is forbidden by El-Sayed rule, e.g., in aromatic compounds,[69]
anthraquinone derivatives,[70] and in multiresonant thermally
activated delayed fluorescence (MR-TADF) dyes, where
the coupling occurs between π π * and nπ * state.[71] For
S1 →T1 transition in the neutral compounds, similar results
are obtained for the rate constants calculated with the FC
and FCHT approaches (see Table S3), with the exception
of S-2(cis), whose computed rate constant with the FCHT
approach likely yields an overestimated value. Under the
computed pieces of evidence, we can conclude that all the
compounds can yield triplet excited states efficiently, in
accordance to the experimental observations. Specifically, we
show that one carbonyl substitution is sufficient for the fast
ISC to happen, further thionation does not increase the ISC
kinetics,[46] and this is in agreement with the study using fsTA spectra.[10,11] We have calculated the transitions to T2 (i.e.,
n-π *) from π –π * singlet state.
We now turn the discussion to the computed T1 →S0
ISC rate constants. Table S4 depicts computed ISC rate
with the FC and FCHT approximations. Interestingly, in this
case, and conversely to the S1 →T1 ISC rate constants, the
effect of including HT effects is mandatory to recover the
experimental trends in the computed triplet excited state
lifetimes. As seen in Table S4, only upon inclusion of HT
effects, we can semiquantitatively recover the experimental
trend in the T1 lifetimes in the series of compounds, i.e.,
decreased T1 lifetimes with increasing degree of thionation.
Still, the computed lifetimes are ca. one order of magnitude
off with respect to the experimental ones, but calculating rate
constants accurately has been proven challenging because
of the inherent complexities[72] of performing these types of
calculations. All in all, this study highlights that the observed
photophysical properties are controlled by a subtle interplay
of the electronic and nuclear degrees of freedom and that
empirical (yet simple) rules, such as, e.g., the heavy-atom
effect and/or the El-Sayed rule are not sufficient to rationalize
the T1 lifetimes in these series of compounds.

PBI compounds. Standard TD-DFT calculations using, e.g.,
the B3LYP functional, find this nπ * state below the π π *
state for S-1. In S-2(cis) and S-2(trans), two nπ * states
are found below the π π *, and in S-3 there are three low
lying nπ * states. Experimentally these nπ * states cannot be
localized due to the strongly allowed transition to the π π *
state. In fact, recent TD-DFT calculations using a specially
tuned range-separated density functional[46] find π π * state
as the lowest state. In order to exclude artifacts caused by
the choice of functional, we tested a wavefunction method,
CAS(10|10) followed by NEVPT2 correlation correction,
using the ORCA 6.0.1 program.[67,68] The π π * state is indeed
strongly stabilized by correlation, more than the nπ * states,
but becomes the S1 state only in combination with a highly
polar solvent (acetonitrile treated by the CPCM model). We
conclude that the nπ * states are close in energy to the π π *
state, and hence considered both states as initial states for
the calculation of ISC rates. These calculations require the
optimized geometries and hessian matrices of the initial and
final states as well as the SOCME and its derivative along
all normal coordinates as input. The adiabatic energy gap
between both states is an adjustable parameter. Hence, we
can use quantum chemical methods that produce reasonable
wavefunctions for all states even if they do not give the perfect
energy ordering.
We have chosen the TD-DFT method with the B3LYP
functional. This produces in all compounds nπ * state as S1 ,
a π π * state for T1 , whereas T2 is of nπ * character. The π π *
singlet state is S2 in compound S-1, S3 in compounds S-2(cis)
and S-2(trans), and S4 in compound S-3. For 1 nπ * initial state,
the largest SOCME are found for the 3 π π * final state, which
is the S1 -T1 transition in all calculations. When we consider
π π * as initial state, the final state with the largest SOCME
is the 3 nπ *, i.e., T2 . Figure S29 shows the calculated ISC rate
constants as function of the energy gap for both alternatives.
For both alternatives we find very large rate constants. The
TDDFT calculations estimate the S1 /T1 gap at ca. 4000 cm−1 .
The corresponding rates are close to 1012 s−1 and are marked
by dots in Figure S29a. In case that the initial state is 1 π π *,
the final state will be T2 (nπ *), i.e., higher in energy. When we
estimate a gap of ca. 3000 cm−1 below the strong absorption
band, the calculations predict rate constants in excess of 1012
s−1 for all compounds, in good agreement with the S1 state
lifetimes of ca. 1 ps. Hence, the fast ISC process cannot
distinguish between nπ * or π π * as the lowest singlet.
