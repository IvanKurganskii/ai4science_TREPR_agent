Thionated Perylenebisimides as Heavy-Atom-Free Triplet
Photosensitizers: Intersystem Crossing, Electron Spin Dynamics, and
Application in Photodynamic Therapy
Xi Chen, Ivan V. Kurganskii, Zhiyong Zhuang, Yue He, Noreen Rehmat, Zafar Mahmood,
Jianzhang Zhao,* Matvey V. Fedin,* Liang Luo,* Daniel Escudero,* and Bernhard Dick*

Abstract: The thionation induced intersystem crossing (ISC) in perylenebisimide (PBI) derivatives was studied. The triplet
state lifetimes of the thionated PBIs are substantially shortened (0.12–0.78 µs) as compared to the unsubstituted PBI (ca.
130 µs). Moreover, there is a clear trend of shortening of the triplet excited state lifetimes and the degree of thionation of
the carbonyl groups in PBI. These findings rationalize the previous report of the contradictory lower 1 O2 quantum yields
for the PBI derivatives with more carbonyl groups thionated.

Importantly we observed ISC for the dianion of the thionated PBI derivatives, and photoexcited dianion species can be
used as super strong reductant for photocatalysis with the advantage of long excited state lifetimes (18–85 µs), and the
excited state oxidation potentials are −1.47 to −1.77 V (versus Fc/Fc+ ).

Introduction
Thionation of carbonyl groups in chromophores has been
known for a long time, and it has been used in a variety of
applications, for instance, to modulate the peptide backbone
conformation.[1–4] It is in particular of interest because this
one-atom substitution is the minimal perturbation of the
chromophore. Recently, it has been used to induce the
intersystem crossing (ISC) ability in organic chromophores.
This strategy has been used with several chromophores
including naphthalimide (NI),[5] naphthaldiimide (NDI),[6,7]
perinone,[8] and also perylenebismide (PBI).[9,10] PBI is a
typical chromophore known for its efficient fluorescence.
Upon thionation of the carbonyl groups, the fluorescence of
the PBI chromophore is literally quenched (F <0.1%).[10]

As the ISC of the thionated PBI is fast (ca. 1 ps, determined by
femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectra),[10,11] the
ISC quantum yields are assumed to be high. Interestingly, it
was found that the singlet oxygen quantum yields ( ) of
the thionated PBI derivatives decrease with the degree of
thionation of the PBI molecule,[11,12], i.e.,  decreased from
95.6% for the monothionated PBI to  = 0% for the PBI
derivative with the four carbonyl groups fully thionated.[13]
This is against the intuition of the putative efficient ISC for
the more thionated PBI derivatives.
Moreover, although the ISC kinetics of the thionated
PBI derivatives has been studied with fs-TA spectra,[10,11]
the triplet state lifetime of the thionated PBI derivatives has
not yet been studied, for instance, with nanosecond transient
absorption (ns-TA) spectroscopy. The triplet state lifetime

in, e.g., photocatalytic and photodynamic therapy (PDT) and

Another interesting aspect concerning the triplet excited
states of the thionated organic chromophores and their
ISC process is the population of the three sublevels of
the triplet state by ISC that is very often overlooked, i.e.,
the different population of the three spin sublevels of the
lowest triplet excited state (T1 ) as a result of the anisotropic
spin–orbit couplings (SOCs) effect. Moreover, the electronic
configuration of the T1 state of the thionated PBI (nπ ∗ or
π π ∗ state) is unknown. The electronic configuration of the
triplet state has a paramount effect on its property, such as
lifetime and the ability for hydrogen abstraction to produce
reactive radicals. Optical spectroscopic methods, such as
steady state or fs/ns-TA spectroscopies, can hardly provide
any information not only about the triplet sublevel selectivity
of the ISC process but also about the spatial distribution and
the electronic configuration of T1 state.
Concerning the above aspects, the pulse laser excited
continuous wave (cw) time-resolved electron paramagnetic
resonance (TREPR) spectroscopy is particularly useful.[14–17]
The non-Boltzmann population of the three sublevels of
the T1 state can be feasibly determined by simulation of
the experimental TREPR spectra, and it is manifested by
the different electron spin polarization (ESP) patterns of the
TREPR spectra, characterized by the enhanced absorption or
emission at the six turning points of the TREPR spectrum,
corresponding to the transitions of the molecules oriented
with the zero field splitting (ZFS) tensor principles axes along
the external magnetic field, i.e., the canonical orientation.

On the other hand, recently, it was proposed the photoexcited anion and dianion form of the PBI derivatives can be
used as super strong reductant in photocatalytic redox organic
reactions. Previously, the monoanion and the dianions of PBI

chromophore have been used for this purpose. However,
the lowest excited state of the monoanion of PBI is doublet
state, for which the excited state lifetime is on the scale
of a few ps, whereas for the dianion, which has a closedshell electronic configuration, the excited state is a singlet
excited state, the lifetime of the lowest excited state is on ns
scale. These short excited state lifetimes of the monoanion
and dianions are actually not long enough for intermolecular
single electron transfer between these photoexcited species
and the photocatalytic reaction substrates. One answer for
this challenge is to find a dianion showing ISC ability, the
triplet excited state of the dianion should show lifetime up to
µs, which will be long enough for the intermolecular electron
transfer. However, to the best of our knowledge, the ISC
of dianion was not reported previously. Herein, we find ISC
ability for the dianion of the thionated PBI compounds.

Results and Discussion
We investigated the photophysical properties of the thionated
PBI derivatives (Scheme 1). To compare the effect of the
thionation, monothionated, dithionated, and trithionated PBI
molecules have been synthesized, respectively (Scheme 1).
Previously the analogue compounds were reported,[10] and
ISC kinetics was studied with fs-TA spectra; however, detailed
photophysical investigations of the compounds were not
reported.

Steady-State Optical Spectra
The UV–vis absorption spectra of the thionated PBI derivatives are shown in Figure 1. The unsubstituted PBI S-0 exhibits
the characteristic structured absorption bands in the range
of 400–550 nm, and the molar absorption coefficient ε ≈
82 000 M−1 cm−1 at 524 nm is consistent with the previously
reported results.[31] Similar absorption spectra were observed
for the thionated PBI derivatives, their molar absorption
coefficients do not significantly differ from S-0 (e.g., ε ≈
69 000 M−1 cm−1 at 571 nm for S-1), however, their absorption wavelengths are redshifted. Specifically, each thionation
causes ca. 50 nm bathochromic shift of the absorption band.
The major absorption band of S-1 is in the range of 400–
600 nm, S-2(cis) and S-2(trans) show major absorption bands
in the range of 300–700 nm, and for S-3 it is between 450–
750 nm (see Figure 1). The redshifted absorption is beneficial
for applications where long wavelength photoexcitation is
beneficial, such as for PDT, photocatalysis, and photovoltaics.
The absorption bands at 400–600 nm are attributed to S0 →S1
transitions (see discussion below). It is worth noting that the
S1 state is not n–π ∗ state because the n–π ∗ state should have a
small molar absorption coefficient due to the poor molecular
orbital overlap.

Previously, the ISC processes of the thionated PBI derivatives
were studied with fs-TA spectra,[10,11] and it was found that
ISC timescale is ca. 1 ps, much faster than the fluorescence
process (which usually takes a few nanoseconds), thus the
ISC quantum yield was assumed to be T ≈ 100%. However,
the triplet state of the thionated PBI was not studied in
detail,[11] for example, the triplet excited state lifetime was
not measured by nanosecond transient absorption spectra. It
should be noted that the approximation of deriving the ISC
quantum yields from the  values is only valid when the
T1 lifetime is long-lived (i.e., >1 µs) and its energy is higher
than the energy of 1 O2 (ca. 0.98 eV). Short triplet excited
state lifetimes will lead to apparently low  because the
photosensitizing of 1 O2 is a diffusion-controlled bimolecular
process in fluid solution. Therefore, it is necessary to study the
triplet excited state lifetimes of the thionated PBI derivatives
with ns-TA spectroscopy to get insights into the quantum
yields of T1 formation. Previously, it was already found
that thionation may reduce the triplet state lifetime of a
chromophore.[7,8,32]

The triplet excited state lifetime of S-1 was determined
to be 0.78 µs by monitoring the decay trace (Figure 2e).
This lifetime is substantially shorter than that of the triplet
excited state of the pristine PBI (without any substituent
attached at the bay position, i.e., τ T = 100–188 µs).[31]
Previously we found that thionation of the NDI moiety
substantially shortens the triplet excited state lifetime.[7,32]
These short triplet excited state lifetimes may be attributed to
the enhanced SOCs between the T1 and S0 state. These results
reveal that the thionation of the carbonyl groups in the chromophore reduces the triplet lifetime, indicating a decrease in
the stability of the triplet state with successive sulfur atom
substitution.[7,32] Therefore, as a general principle, we propose
that the observable triplet state lifetime of a chromophore
is actually dependent on the ISC mechanism responsible for
producing the triplet excited state.[33−35] For instance, the
lifetime of the triplet state produced by the heavy atom effect
is usually shorter than the intrinsic triplet state lifetime of the
chromophore because not only the S1 →T1 but also the T1 →S0
transition can be enhanced by the heavy atom effect.
Similar ns-TA spectral features were observed for other
thionated PBI derivatives (Figure 2b–d). We found that the
regioisomers S-2(trans) and S-2(cis) show slightly different
triplet excited state lifetimes of 0.35 and 0.32 µs, respectively.

Moreover, we found that the triplet excited state lifetime is
shortened with the degree of thionation, for instance, for S3, with three carbonyl groups thionated, its triplet excited
state lifetime is shortened up to 0.12 µs (Figure 2h). In
comparison, S-1 possess a triplet excited state lifetime of
0.78 µs (Figure 2e). We found that thionation of the NDI
chromophore with amino substituents attached at the bay
position increases the  values significantly (from 7.0% to
56%). However, the triplet excited state lifetime of the amino
substituted NDI chromophore is shortened (from 51.4 to 14.4
µs).[7] Thionated PBI with amino-substituents attached at the
bay position have been used as photothermal agents (PTAs).
Similar results were observed for the thionated PBI with
phenyl-substituents attached at the bay positions.[13,32]
These results agree with the previously observed 
values of the thionated PBI derivatives, that is, lower 
was observed for the derivative containing more thionated
carbonyl groups.[13] This effect is due to their short triplet
excited state lifetimes, which will lead to poor quenching
effect on the 3 PBI by O2 , a diffusion-controlled process.
This hypothesis is supported by the O.D. values of the
ns-TA spectra of the derivatives (Figure 2), which do not
decrease substantially with increasing degree of thionation
of the PBI chromophore. In general, short triplet excited
state lifetimes are actually detrimental to the application of
the photosensitizers in PDT,[36,37] triplet–triplet annihilation
upconversion,[38−41] and photocatalysis[42−45] because with
short triplet state lifetimes, the intermolecular triplet–triplet
energy transfer or electron transfer may be attenuated. Thus,
we propose that one should carefully choose the degree of
thionation of the carbonyl groups as a method to enhance ISC
to design heavy-atom-triplet photosensitizers, to be used in
PDT and photocatalytic redox organic reactions, etc.

The ISC processes of the thionated PBI derivatives have
been studied with fs-TA spectra.[10] The results show that
the ISC kinetics (0.59–0.94 ps) do not vary substantially with
the degree of thionation.[10] The triplet energy level of S1 is 1.10 eV,[11,46] and the singlet energy level is 1.77 eV.[6]
These results also support our postulate that the ISC quantum
yields of the thionated PBI derivative do not decrease with
increasing of the thionation extent, although the apparent 
decrease by increasing the thionation extent (Table 1). These
PBI derivatives show efficient ISC due to the thionation of the
carbonyl groups in the compound.
However, the triplet state lifetimes of the compounds are
short, τ S-1 = 0.78 µs, τ S-2(trans) = 0.35 µs, τ S-2(cis) = 0.32 µs, and
τ S-3 = 0.12 µs. This is interesting because these triplet state
lifetimes are unusually short. The spin sublevel selectivity
of this efficient ISC has not yet been investigated, and the
electronic configuration of the T1 state of the thionated
PBI (nπ ∗ or π π ∗ state) is unknown. The ordinary optical
spectroscopic methods, e.g., fs/ns-TA spectra, cannot give this
information, therefore, we performed time-resolved electron
paramagnetic resonance spectral study along with quantum
chemical investigations.

observed, simulation of the spectrum gives the g factor of
2.0036, and the two equivalent N atoms give a hyperfine
interaction constants (HFI) of 1.87 MHz; however, the HFI
constants of the H atoms on the PBI chromophores indicate
that spin distributions are unequal (Table 4). For the monoanion of S-1, the two N atoms give different HFI constants;
therefore, the two N atoms are no longer equivalent. Note,
the g value of [S-1]•− is 2.0059, which is larger than that of
[S-0]•− (2.0036). This is reasonable as g value is dependent on
the SOC magnitude.[14] For the monoanion of the dithionated
PBI, i.e., [S-2(cis)]•− and [S-2(trans)]•− , the two N atoms are

equivalent for the electron spin density distribution. This is
in agreement with the symmetry of the molecular structures
(Scheme 1).
Note the g value is [S-2(cis)]•− larger than [S-0]•− and
[S-1]•− . Interestingly, for [S-3]•− , the HFI coupling constants
show that the two N atoms become nonequivalent, and
the g value is the largest among the few derivatives, which
is in agreement with largest SOC for this triple thionated
compound.[64]
These negatively charged species are in particular of
interests because the photoexcited states of these monoanion
and dianions are super strong reductants in photocatalytic
redox organic reactions. However, a road blocks for these
photoexcited anions to be used in photocatalysis is their shortexcited state lifetimes. For the monoanions, the excited state
(doublet state) lifetimes are on the picoseconds magnitude.
For the dianions, with closed shell electronic configuration,
the excited state (normally singlet excited state) lifetimes
are on the scales of a few nanoseconds. These excited state
lifetimes are too short for molecular diffusion in fluid solution
at a reasonable concentration.
Thus, using CW EPR, we have observed a correlation
between g values and the number of S atoms in the molecule.
Larger deviation of the g factor from that of the free electron
implies a larger contribution of SOC effect. Thus, we observe
that the incorporation of additional S atoms increases the
SOC within the compound. Notably, S substitution at distinct
positions renders the two N atoms in the PBI molecule
nonequivalent, and this nonuniform spin density distribution
is capable of augmenting the chemical reactivity of the
molecule. Further details on how this affects the ISC can be
found in the section of theoretical study (see below).
In order to fully exploit the advantages of the super
strong reductant potential of the anions, herein, we studied
the formation of the monoanions and the dianions of the
thionated PBI derivatives, and we found the ISC ability for
the dianions.

Density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT
(TD-DFT) investigations were performed to provide in-depth
rationalization of the observed properties (see details in the
Supporting Information). The electron spin density plots of
the T1 states for the thionated PBI derivatives are presented
in Figure 8, clearly displaying a predominant 3 π –π ∗ character

found the electron spin delocalization overcomes the spin
polarization.[65]
Previous theoretical analysis based on natural bond orbital
(NBO) analysis show that there is a poor overlap between
the lone pairs of the sulfur and the carbon atoms, and as a
result, the antibonding character of π *C = S is weaker than
that of π *C = O , leading to a lower LUMO energy level for
the thiocarbonyl group than for the carbonyl group.[66] This
effect contributes to the anodically shifted reduction potential
of the thionated PBI derivatives as compared to the oxo
counterpart, as well as for the redshifted absorption bands of
the thionated PBI derivatives.

the S1 state shows nπ ∗ character for all compounds with
sulfur atoms. As a consequence, the SOC of the transition
between S1 /T1 is large. In first step, we calculated the ISC
rate constants for the transitions S1 →T1 and T1 →S0 in the
neutral compounds with the ESD (PBE0/6-311G**) module
implemented in the ORCA program[67,68] at fixed values of
the energy gap (adiabatic energy gap calculated by SCSADC(2)). The results are listed in Tables S3 and S4. In
second step, these ISC rate were calculated for the neutral
compounds and their dianions as a function of the energy gap
with a home-written program (see Supporting Information)
that takes the hessians, spin–orbit coupling matrix elements
(SOCMEs), and their derivatives with respect to the normal
coordinates as input. In both cases, the Franck–Condon (FC)
contribution as well as the Herzberg–Teller (HT) contribution
was calculated.
It has been shown that HT contributions are the main
driving force of ISC processes occurring where the transition
is forbidden by El-Sayed rule, e.g., in aromatic compounds,[69]
anthraquinone derivatives,[70] and in multiresonant thermally
activated delayed fluorescence (MR-TADF) dyes, where
the coupling occurs between π π * and nπ * state.[71] For
S1 →T1 transition in the neutral compounds, similar results
are obtained for the rate constants calculated with the FC
and FCHT approaches (see Table S3), with the exception
of S-2(cis), whose computed rate constant with the FCHT
approach likely yields an overestimated value. Under the
computed pieces of evidence, we can conclude that all the
compounds can yield triplet excited states efficiently, in
accordance to the experimental observations. Specifically, we
show that one carbonyl substitution is sufficient for the fast
ISC to happen, further thionation does not increase the ISC
kinetics,[46] and this is in agreement with the study using fsTA spectra.[10,11] We have calculated the transitions to T2 (i.e.,
n-π *) from π –π * singlet state.
We now turn the discussion to the computed T1 →S0
ISC rate constants. Table S4 depicts computed ISC rate
with the FC and FCHT approximations. Interestingly, in this
case, and conversely to the S1 →T1 ISC rate constants, the
effect of including HT effects is mandatory to recover the
experimental trends in the computed triplet excited state
lifetimes. As seen in Table S4, only upon inclusion of HT
effects, we can semiquantitatively recover the experimental
trend in the T1 lifetimes in the series of compounds, i.e.,
decreased T1 lifetimes with increasing degree of thionation.
Still, the computed lifetimes are ca. one order of magnitude
off with respect to the experimental ones, but calculating rate
constants accurately has been proven challenging because
of the inherent complexities[72] of performing these types of
calculations. All in all, this study highlights that the observed
photophysical properties are controlled by a subtle interplay
of the electronic and nuclear degrees of freedom and that
empirical (yet simple) rules, such as, e.g., the heavy-atom
effect and/or the El-Sayed rule are not sufficient to rationalize
the T1 lifetimes in these series of compounds.
The lone pair on the C═S group has a lower ionization
potential than that on the C═O group. This lowers the
energy of the corresponding nπ * state in the thionated

PBI compounds. Standard TD-DFT calculations using, e.g.,
the B3LYP functional, find this nπ * state below the π π *
state for S-1. In S-2(cis) and S-2(trans), two nπ * states
are found below the π π *, and in S-3 there are three low
lying nπ * states. Experimentally these nπ * states cannot be
localized due to the strongly allowed transition to the π π *
state. In fact, recent TD-DFT calculations using a specially
tuned range-separated density functional[46] find π π * state
as the lowest state. In order to exclude artifacts caused by
the choice of functional, we tested a wavefunction method,
CAS(10|10) followed by NEVPT2 correlation correction,
using the ORCA 6.0.1 program.[67,68] The π π * state is indeed
strongly stabilized by correlation, more than the nπ * states,
but becomes the S1 state only in combination with a highly
polar solvent (acetonitrile treated by the CPCM model). We
conclude that the nπ * states are close in energy to the π π *
state, and hence considered both states as initial states for
the calculation of ISC rates. These calculations require the
optimized geometries and hessian matrices of the initial and
final states as well as the SOCME and its derivative along
all normal coordinates as input. The adiabatic energy gap
between both states is an adjustable parameter. Hence, we
can use quantum chemical methods that produce reasonable
wavefunctions for all states even if they do not give the perfect
energy ordering.
We have chosen the TD-DFT method with the B3LYP
functional. This produces in all compounds nπ * state as S1 ,
a π π * state for T1 , whereas T2 is of nπ * character. The π π *
singlet state is S2 in compound S-1, S3 in compounds S-2(cis)
and S-2(trans), and S4 in compound S-3. For 1 nπ * initial state,
the largest SOCME are found for the 3 π π * final state, which
is the S1 -T1 transition in all calculations. When we consider
π π * as initial state, the final state with the largest SOCME
is the 3 nπ *, i.e., T2 . Figure S29 shows the calculated ISC rate
constants as function of the energy gap for both alternatives.
For both alternatives we find very large rate constants. The
TDDFT calculations estimate the S1 /T1 gap at ca. 4000 cm−1 .
The corresponding rates are close to 1012 s−1 and are marked
by dots in Figure S29a. In case that the initial state is 1 π π *,
the final state will be T2 (nπ *), i.e., higher in energy. When we
estimate a gap of ca. 3000 cm−1 below the strong absorption
band, the calculations predict rate constants in excess of 1012
s−1 for all compounds, in good agreement with the S1 state
lifetimes of ca. 1 ps. Hence, the fast ISC process cannot
distinguish between nπ * or π π * as the lowest singlet.

These theoretical insights demonstrate the intricate interplay between electronic and nuclear factors governing the
photophysical behavior of thionated PBI derivatives. Specifically, a single carbonyl substitution enables rapid ISC,
whereas additional thionation does not significantly enhance
ISC kinetics. Energy gap calculations reveal differences in
EST between neutral and dianionic forms, with thionation
generally reducing EST in neutral compounds but increasing
it in dianions. Furthermore, spin density analysis indicates
that thionation significantly influences electron delocalization, with asymmetric thionation leading to uneven electron
distribution and consequently enhanced reactivity.

that they are nearly nontoxic. S-1-liposomes were selected

clearly observed accompanying the generation of ROS while
pretreating the HeLa cells with both S-1-liposomes and
DCF-DA, whereas HeLa cells incubated by only DCF-DA

which also confirmed that the cells died due to the PDT
effect of S-1-liposomes.These study highlights the potential of
perylenebismide-based PSs for PDT applications, providing
insights for designing future heavy-atom-free triplet PSs.

In summary, we studied the ISC and the triplet excited state
property in a series perylenebisimide (PBI) derivatives in
which the carbonyl groups are thionated. The ISC of both
the neutral form and the dianionic form of these compounds
were studied. The purpose of the study is to unveil the
effect of thionation on the photophysics of chromophores
and to explore the ISC ability of the dianions, so that the
photoexcited dianions (for which the long-lived triplet state
is populated, in contrast to the previously reported shortlived doublet excited state of the monoanion or the singlet
excited state of the dianions) can be used as efficient super
strong reductant in photocatalytic redox organic reactions.
With nanosecond transient absorption spectra, we found that
the triplet excited state lifetime of the neutral thionated
PBI derivatives gradually shortens from 0.78 µs for the
monothionated PBI to 0.35 µs, 0.32 µs, and 0.12 µs for the
bisthionated and triplethionated PBI, respectively. All these
lifetimes are much shorter than the lifetime of the T1 state of
the unsubstituted PBI chromophore (ca. 127 µs). This short
lifetime is responsible for the low  of these dyes, and
these unusually short triplet state lifetimes are detrimental
to the application of these dyes as photosensitizers because
the intermolecular triplet energy transfer or electron transfer
becomes less efficient with shorter triplet state lifetimes.
These results demonstrate the thionation of the carbonyl
groups in a chromophore to induce ISC is probably not an
ideal molecular structure design method for triplet photosensitizers to be used in the areas such as PDT because the triplet
excited state lifetime of the chromophore can be substantially
shortened.

These thionated PBI derivatives were used
for photodynamic therapy. Moreover, we observed ISC for
the dianions of the thionated PBI derivatives, the triplet state
