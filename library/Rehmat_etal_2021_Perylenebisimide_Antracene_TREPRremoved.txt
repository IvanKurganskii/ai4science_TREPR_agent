a spin-forbidden process. Different approaches have been
used to induce ISC in aromatic compounds. ISC can be enhanced by using heavy atoms (I, Br, Pt, Ir, Ru),[8, 27–32] but the
presence of these atoms may shorten the triplet-state lifetime
or increase the cost of materials.[33, 34] As alternatives, electron
spin conversion,[35–38] doubly excited states,[39, 40] and exciton
coupling were also used to induce ISC in organic compounds,[41–43] but the molecular structures required for these
methods are usually synthetically demanding.
One promising approach to populate the triplet excited
state of organic compounds without any heavy atom is charge
recombination (CR)-induced ISC in electron donor–acceptor
systems.[44–48] Charge separation (CS) in electron donor–acceptor dyads leads to formation of a charge transfer (CT) state,
which can either decay to the ground state or may populate
the triplet state through a CR process. When the donor and acceptor are separated by a large distance, the electronic exchange energy is negligible (J < 0.01 cm@1) and the 3CT state is
populated by a radical-pair ISC (RP-ISC) mechanism, then the
internal conversion will produce the 3LE state.[49–51] The main
driving force in RP-ISC is the hyperfine coupling interaction,
and the ISC is slow. Usually, RP-ISC requires a weak coupling interaction, and thus a long spacer between donor and acceptor,
which is synthetically demanding.[52] Therefore, the conventional electron donor–acceptor dyads are not ideal triplet photosensitizers.

On the other hand, the RP-ISC in compact electron donor–
acceptor dyads is inhibited, due to the large electronic coupling between the donor and acceptor moieties. However, if
the electron donor and acceptor are in orthogonal geometry,
the triplet exited state will be populated through spin–orbit
charge-transfer ISC (SOCT-ISC).[44, 53–57] In this process, CR between donor and acceptor produces a large change in molecular orbit angular momentum. This change in momentum produces a torque which causes electron spin flipping or rephrasing, and the molecular orbital angular momentum change is
off-set by the electron spin angular momentum change, and
thus ISC is enhanced.[53] The SOCT-ISC process occurs in few picoseconds, whereas RP-ISC is a comparatively slow process
that occurs on nanosecond timescales. Visible light-harvesting
chromophores such as anthracene,[53] bodipy,[44] and perylene[58] have been used for the design of SOCT-ISC compact
electron donor–acceptor dyads. However, it is clear that the
chromophores for construction of compact electron donor–acceptor dyads need to be diversified, and the relationship between the molecular structure and the SOCT-ISC needs to be
fully revealed. For instance, it was proposed that an intermediate 3LE state (LE: locally excited) having similar energy to the
CT state is essential to SOCT-ISC, but experimental evidence for
this postulate is limited.
In this respect, perylenebisimide (PBI) is an interesting chromophore due to its excellent thermal and optical stabilities,[59, 60] high molar absorption coefficient,[61] reversible redox
properties,[48, 62–65] and feasible derivatization chemistry.[66]
These features make PBI a versatile chromophore in the field
of photovoltaics,[67] artificial photosynthesis,[68] and CS studies.[64, 69–72] However, most of the previous studies focused on

the fluorescent properties of the PBI chromophore, and its
triplet-state behavior remains unrevealed to a large extent. Previously, PBI–phenothioazine (PTZ) and PBI-diphenylamine
(DPA) dyads were prepared.[73] Unfortunately, no triplet-state
population was observed for PBI-PTZ.
Inspired by these studies, we prepared compact PBI-AN
dyads and triad by linking the AN moiety with a PBI chromophore in different orientations (Scheme 1). The rationale for
this molecular design is to control the conformation between
the electron donor and acceptor (PBI-9-AN, PBI-2-AN, PBI-Bis2-AN; Scheme 1). As reference compounds, phenyl-substituted
PBIs (PBI-Ph and PBI-Bis-Ph) were also synthesized. AN acts as
electron donor, while PBI is a strong electron acceptor. Electrochemical studies predicted an efficient electron-transfer process even in nonpolar solvents such as n-hexane. We found
that the chromophore orientation affects the photophysical
properties of the compound, such as absorption, emission, and
triplet-state population. Moreover, we found reversible photoreduction of the dyads to produce PBI radical anion (PBIC@)
and dianion (PBI2@) in the presence of a sacrificial electron
donor triethanolamine (TEOA). We demonstrated that selective
excitation into the near-IR absorption bands of PBIC@ does not
produce PBI2@.

than the T1 state of the PBI moiety (ca. 1.2 eV), and thus attachment of AN may allow for insertion of the crucial intermediate Tn state for enhancing SOCT-ISC.

Steady state UV/Vis absorption and fluorescence spectra
The UV/Vis absorption spectra of the PBI-AN dyads/triad and
reference compounds in n-hexane were studied (Figure 2). The
native PBI exhibits the characteristic structured absorption in
the range of 400–550 nm, while reference compounds PBI-Ph
and PBI-Bis-Ph show a slightly redshifted absorptions (519 and
537 nm, respectively) compared with unsubstituted PBI
(512 nm). For PBI-9-AN, the absorption spectrum is not simply
the sum of absorption of PBI and AN moieties (Figure 2 a).
First, the molar absorption coefficient of PBI-9-AN is only half
that of PBI. Second, weak coupling in the ground state between the AN and PBI moieties was observed, which causes
the appearance of a weak but distinctive shoulder absorption
band in the range of 500–600 nm. In the case of PBI-2-AN and
PBI-Bis-2-AN, the absorption spectra show bathochromic shift
compared with references PBI-Ph (519 nm) and PBI-Bis-Ph
(537 nm). The broad structureless absorption band of PBI-Bis2-AN indicates the existence of ground-state electronic coupling between AN and PBI moieties.
The fluorescence of the dyads/triad along with their reference compounds was studied (Figure 3, Figures S18–S20 in the
Supporting Information). In a nonpolar solvent such as nhexane, all the derivatives show weak and redshifted emission,
whereas the native PBI moiety is highly fluorescent (optically
matched solutions were used). In the case of PBI-2-AN, only
one redshifted emission band centered at 580 nm was observed, and no separate 1LE emission band of the PBI moiety,
which usually appears at 520 nm, was observed. This redshifted emission band can be assigned to a 1CT!S0 transition. For
PBI-2-AN, the LE emission of the PBI moiety is almost
quenched or merged with the CT emission band. For PBI-Bis2-AN, symmetry-breaking CT is expected for quenching of the
fluorescence.[74, 75]
In polar solvents, the fluorescence of the dyads is generally
quenched (Figure 3 b and c), but the fluorescence intensity of
PBI-Ph remains unchanged in different solvents (Figure 3 d). In

unit), 49 % singlet-oxygen quantum yield was observed, while
only 20 % singlet oxygen quantum yield was observed on exciting the AN unit at 355 nm. A possible reason for different
singlet-oxygen quantum yields at different excitation wavelengths may be an inefficient FRET process or the existence of
other decay channels on excitation at short wavelength

Nanosecond transient absorption spectroscopy: triplet-state
properties
PBI is a highly fluorescent chromophore (FF = 99 % in nhexane). However, preliminary results showed that singletoxygen production is significant for the dyads/triad (Table 2);
for instance, the singlet-oxygen quantum yield (FD) of PBI-9AN is 84 % in n-hexane, and for PBI-2-AN, FD is 57 % (in toluene). Thus, we propose that ISC is efficient for PBI-AN dyads/

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy
To elucidate the kinetics of the photophysical processes, femtosecond transient absorption measurements on PBI-AN
dyads/triad were carried out (Figure 7). Ultrafast pump–probe

state of the PBI chromophore is populated, as is evident by
the appearance of GSB centered at 540 nm, stimulated emission (SE) in the range of 585–650 nm, and a broad ESA band
centered at 740 nm (Figure 7 a). Within a few picoseconds
(10 ps), the SE disappears, the ESA band at 740 nm further
rises in intensity, and also a broad ESA band centered at 675
appears, which can be assigned to a charge-transfer process.
The charge transfer appears to be a biexponential process, as
at further long time delay (51 ps), an additional rise in the intensity of ESA band was observed. The charge recombination
process takes place on a longer timescale in the range of few
nanoseconds, and the triplet excited state of PBI is populated.
A similar photophysical process was observed in PBI-Bis-2-AN

with slow charge separation, which takes place within 60 ps
(Figure S30 in the Supporting Information).

fluorescence quantum yield and high ISC ability. The electrochemical analysis also confirmed the strong PET process in

Conclusion
We prepared PBI-AN compact electron donor–acceptor dyads/
triad to study the effect of molecular conformation and chromophore orientation on the photophysical properties, especially the SOCT-ISC efficiency. The molecular conformation was
varied by changing the substitution position of the AN moiety
attached at the bay position of the PBI moiety. Weaker
ground-state electronic coupling between the PBI and AN
moieties was observed in the dyad with substitution at the 9position of the AN moiety, which adopts nearly orthogonal geometry. In contrast, in the dyad and triad with substitution at
the 2-position of the AN unit, a more coplanar geometry resulted, as well as stronger ground-state coupling and redshifted absorption. The orthogonal dyad shows more efficient triplet population and high singlet-oxygen generation (FD = 86 %)
compared with the non-orthogonal dyad (FD = 57 %). All the
dyads/triad show the long-lived PBI-localized triplet state (tT =
139 ms). Femtosecond transient absorption spectra show that
the charge separation of PBI-2-AN takes 3.6 ps, and the charge
recombination (i.e., SOCT-ISC) occurs within 2.8 ns.

All the PBI derivatives exhibit efficient reversible
formation of the near IR-absorbing PBI radical anion (PBIC@ , absorptions bands centered at 708, 802, 864, and 954 nm) on
photoreduction in the presence of a sacrificial electron donor
(TEOA). For PBI-AN triad, we observed reversible stepwise photoreduction of the PBI moiety in the triad to the radical anion
(PBIC@) and dianion (PBI2@) in the presence of TEOA. PBI2@ is
potent reductant (oxidation potential of PBI2@ : @1.29 V vs. Fc/
Fc + ) and could be used in photovoltaics and photoredox catalytic synthetic organic reactions. A detailed mechanistic study
on the stepwise photoreduction with monochromatic photoirradiation indicated that selective photoexcitation of PBIC@ at
the near-IR absorption band (centered at 740 nm), mainly D0 !

www.chemeurj.org

D4 transition in the presence of TEOA, does not produce PBI2@.
Instead, formation of PBI2@ was observed by excitation of the
PBIC@ at shorter wavelength (< 520 nm). Our results are useful
for design of new heavy-atom-free triplet photosensitizers and
study of the electron spin polarization of the ISC, as well as
the photo-driven electron transfer.

Nanosecond transient absorption spectroscopy
An LP980 laser flash photolysis spectrometer (Edinburg Instruments, UK) equipped with Tektronix TDS 3012B oscilloscope was
used to study the nano- and microsecond dynamics. The samples
were deaerated with N2 for 15 min prior to investigations and excited with a nanosecond pulsed laser (Opolette, wavelength tunable in the range of 210–2400 nm, OPOTEK, USA). The typical laser
power was approximately 5 mJ per pulse. The data were processed
with L900 software. Note that the triplet state lifetimes presented
in the manuscript are “apparent” values, which are highly dependent on the experimental conditions.

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy
The femtosecond transient absorption spectra were measured in a
homemade pump–probe setup in combination with a modelocked Ti-sapphire amplified laser system (Spitfire Ace, SpectraPhysics). The system was amplified with a wavelength of 800 nm
of duration 35 fs, repetition rate 1 kHz, and average power 4 W.
The output beam was then split into two parts; 90 % of the beam
after frequency conversion in optical parametric oscillator (Topas,
Light Conversion) into UV-VIS-IR in the range of 240–2400 nm was
used as pump beam. The remaining 10 % output beam was used
to generate a white light continuum in a 3 mm-thick rotated CaF2
plate and served as probe beam. The probe beam was passed
through a variable delay line (up to 6 ns), and its absorbance
change in the presence and in the absence of the pump beam
was measured. The magic angle between pump- and probe-beam
polarization directions was set in order to avoid rotational depolarization effects. The entire setup was controlled by a PC with the
help of LabView software (National Instruments). Global target
analyses were carried out with the software Glotaran.

each sample was shock-frozen in liquid nitrogen, quickly transferred into the probe of the spectrometer precooled at 80 K, and
then allowed to undergo temperature equilibration over several
minutes. All simulations were performed with the EasySpin package for MatLab.[83]

DFT computations
The geometries of the compounds were optimized by DFT with
B3LYP functional and 6-31G(d) basis set. The excitation energy and
energy gaps between S0 state and excited triplet states of the
compounds were approximated on the basis of the optimal
ground-state geometry. All these calculations were performed with
Gaussian 09W.[105]

Photoreduction experiment
A 35 W xenon lamp (25 mW cm@2) was used to conduct the photoreduction experiment. Triethanolamine (TEOA) was used as sacrificial electron donor. An Agilent 8453 UV/Vis spectrometer was employed to record the absorption spectra. The samples (compound + TEOA) were deaerated with N2 for 10 min prior to photoirradiation. In order to elucidate the mechanism of formation of
PBI2@ by photo-reduction, 730 nm LED (5W) and 520 nm LED (7W)
were used to irradiate a solution of PBIC@ .
